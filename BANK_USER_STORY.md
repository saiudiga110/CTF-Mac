# Just an Ordinary Tuesday

Priya opens the vBank app the way she opens it every morning — half-asleep, coffee in one hand, thumb already knowing where the login button is. She doesn't remember the last time she actually looked at the screen while typing her password. It's just muscle memory now: username, password, in.

Today, oddly, it lets her in even faster than usual. She doesn't think anything of it. Banks are supposed to be fast now, aren't they? That's the whole pitch — everything instant, everything at your fingertips.

Her balance is right where she left it. Good. She scrolls down to Statements, mostly out of habit, checking nothing in particular. There's a little box on the screen where you can type in an account number to look something up — she's never really used it, but this morning her thumb lingers on it. On a whim, she changes the number to something else, just curious what happens.

What happens is someone else's bank statement. Not a stranger's, either — a name she recognises, someone senior, someone whose signature is probably on the letter that welcomed her as a customer three years ago. Six-figure numbers. A note in one of the entries that reads more like a warning than a memo — something about a payment that got stopped, an account that isn't supposed to be paid ever again.

She should probably tell someone. She makes a note to mention it to the helpline, then gets distracted, because there's a payment she actually needs to make.

She goes to Send Money. Habit again: pick a saved payee, type an amount, done. But that account she saw a minute ago is still sitting in her head, and there's a little link for paying someone new. She types in the details — the ones from that other statement, if she's honest with herself, more curiosity than anything else — and the app tells her, quite plainly, that this particular payment is on hold. Flagged. Under review.

Fine, she thinks. Makes sense.

Except somehow, a moment later, it isn't on hold anymore. The screen just says the payment went through. She stares at it for a second too long before shrugging and moving on — technology, she thinks, who really understands it.

There's a small issue with her card that's been bothering her for a week, so she opens a support message and types out exactly what's wrong, the way she always does — long, detailed, a little frustrated. She hits send and doesn't think about it again. She has no way of knowing that somewhere, a member of staff will open that message later today, and that opening it will hand a piece of their own session to whoever wrote it. She just wanted her card fixed.

Later, waiting for the kettle, she remembers a bill that's due today and almost forgot. There's a faster way to send money — Express, the app calls it, for exactly this kind of morning. She sends it. Then, because her hands are quicker than her patience, she sends it again before the first one has even finished loading, half by accident, tapping the button twice. The app doesn't seem to mind. Neither does she, until she checks her balance and it's lower than the maths should allow. She frowns, decides she must have miscounted, and closes the app for a minute.

Before she leaves for work, she downloads a receipt for her records — the app calls it encrypted, which sounds reassuring, a padlock icon and everything. She doesn't open the file. Why would she? It's just a receipt. Somewhere in the part of the bank she'll never see, that same word — encrypted — is doing a lot less than the padlock icon suggests.

That's the whole of Priya's Tuesday, banking-wise. Nothing dramatic happened to her. Nobody called her, nobody warned her, nothing on her screen ever once said *something is wrong here*. She saw a stranger's statement because a box let her type in any number she liked. She sent money to an account the bank itself had already decided not to trust, because a screen said "on hold" and then, a moment later, un-said it. She wrote an honest message to support and it went further than she meant it to. She tapped a button twice and the bank quietly lost track of what she actually owed.

None of it felt like anything. That's really the whole point of it. A customer's Tuesday is supposed to feel like nothing — log in, check balance, send money, get on with your day. Every one of those small, unremarkable moments is exactly where someone would need to be looking, if they wanted to understand how much this bank was quietly willing to take on trust.

Somewhere else in the building — not anywhere Priya has ever been, not anywhere she has a reason to go — there's a much smaller room with a much bigger problem, the kind that doesn't show up in anyone's statement at all. But that's not her story. Hers is just an ordinary Tuesday, and that's exactly what makes it worth telling.
